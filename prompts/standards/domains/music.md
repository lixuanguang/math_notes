# Music Domain Overlay

Use this overlay for music theory and analysis, historical musicology, ethnomusicology, composition, notation, performance practice, recordings, and digital score representation.

## Source and musical identity

- Classify evidence as autograph/manuscript, edition/score, notation example, theoretical construct, performance, recording, testimony/field record, or scholarly interpretation. Keep notated, editorial, inferred, and performed features distinct.
- Identify creator/composer or attribution, work/title and catalogue number, movement/section, version/arrangement, editor/arranger, edition/date, instrumentation/voices, and location. Cite measures/rehearsal marks/beats/pages or manuscript folios.
- For recordings, record performers/ensemble/conductor, venue/date when known, label/release and track, medium/remaster/version, and timestamp. A recording documents one performance and production, not the work in the abstract.

## Notation and analysis

- State pitch-naming and octave system, written versus sounding/concert pitch, transposition, tuning/temperament, clef, key/mode, meter, tempo, rhythmic or mensural convention, and instrument-specific notation when they affect interpretation.
- Define analytical systems before use—Roman-numeral convention, figured bass, pitch-class/set notation, scale-degree symbols, form labels, transformational or Schenkerian notation—and do not mix incompatible systems silently.
- An analysis identifies the exact musical events and the inference to harmony, voice leading, rhythm/meter, motive, form, timbre, texture, orchestration, topic/style, or performance choice. Mark alternative hearings and avoid treating an analytical label as self-evident evidence.
- Music examples include enough clef/key/meter, surrounding context, measure numbers, parts/voices, and legend to be readable. Distinguish source notation from editorial and analytical overlays; render-inspect staff size, symbols, beams, lyrics, labels, and collisions and provide a concise textual alternative.

## Editions, encodings, and context

- For source-critical work, identify witnesses, variant readings, editorial additions, normalization, and performance-practice assumptions. For non-Western, oral, or community traditions, state transcription choices, tuning/rhythm limits, performer/community terminology, permissions, and the relation between transcription and event.
- For MEI, MusicXML, or other digital notation, record format/profile and version, source-to-encoding policy, IDs/metadata, score/part and facsimile/recording links, validation result, and known loss on import/export. Schema validity does not replace visual and musical comparison with the source.
- Record copyright/licence and credit for scores, recordings, and examples; quotation or educational purpose does not automatically settle permission or fair-use questions.
- Load physics for acoustics, history for archival context, linguistics for sung language, and computer science for MIR or encoding systems.
